Slide 6: Understanding Home Staging

Module one: Understanding Home Staging. To use staging well, you first need to understand it. We look at what it is and is not, the different types, how buyers really look at a home, what agents report, and what the process involves.

Slide 7: Buyers decide with their eyes first.

Why does this matter to you? Because buyers decide with their eyes first. Most meet a property through photographs, long before they stand in the hall. They scroll past many homes, and the ones that look ready, light and cared for are the ones that stop the scroll. Emotion comes before logic: a buyer who can picture living in the home has already begun to imagine buying it. Your listing is judged in those first seconds, so it pays to understand exactly what makes a home look ready. That is what staging is for.

Slide 8: What home staging is

Let's define it. Home staging is preparing a property so that it appeals to the widest range of buyers and shows at its best, in photographs and in person. That means repairs, cleaning and decluttering, rooms that are neutral, well lit and well arranged, and furniture and accessories chosen for the buyer rather than the owner. It is not redecorating to the owner's taste. It is never about hiding problems or misleading a buyer, because a staged home should always be honest about what it is. And it is not only for empty homes or expensive ones. A well-prepared home at any price point shows better than an unprepared one.

Slide 9: The types of staging

There are four main types of staging. A consultation is where a professional visits and gives the owner a written plan to follow. It is the lowest-cost way in. Occupied staging is for homes where the owner still lives, and the home is prepared around their life, often with what they already own. Vacant staging is for empty properties, where furniture and accessories are brought in so buyers can see the scale and purpose of each room. Virtual staging adds furniture to photographs digitally. It can be very useful for marketing, but it must always be clearly disclosed, because a buyer who visits will find an empty room.

Slide 10: How buyers look at a home

Follow the journey of a buyer. Online: the first photographs decide whether they click or move on. At the viewing: the first moments in the hall set the tone for everything else. When comparing: after a day of viewings, buyers remember the homes that felt easy to live in, not the ones with the longest feature list. And when deciding: feeling comes first, and the numbers are used to justify it afterwards. Staging works at all four moments, which is why it is such a powerful marketing tool.

Slide 11: What agents report

Now the evidence. The National Association of Realtors in the United States regularly surveys its members about staging. In its Profile of Home Staging, eighty-three percent of buyers' agents said staging made it easier for a buyer to picture the property as a future home. Twenty-nine percent of sellers' agents reported an increase of between one and ten percent in the dollar value offered on staged homes. And forty-nine percent of sellers' agents said staging reduced the time a home spent on the market. Please note the wording. These are agents' reports from a survey in the United States, not guarantees, and results vary by market and property. Use them as supporting evidence, not as promises.

Slide 12: Using the figures honestly

Using these figures honestly protects your credibility. Say things like, agents in a US survey report, or, many agents find that staging helps buyers picture the home. Avoid promising a higher price or a sale in a week, and never quote a figure without its source. Owners trust an agent who is careful with claims, and in many places there are rules about what you may say in marketing. Your own local results will become your strongest evidence, and in module three we look at how to track them.

Slide 13: The staging process from start to finish

Here is the process from start to finish, so you know what you are asking an owner to do. Consult: the stager visits and assesses each room. Plan: the owner receives a written plan and a quote. Prepare: repairs, painting, a deep clean and decluttering. Style: furniture and accessories are arranged room by room. Photograph: professional photographs are taken once the home is ready. Show: viewings follow, and then the staging is removed or the home sells. You do not manage this process, but understanding it lets you explain it clearly to owners and plan your listing date around it.

Slide 14: Where staging counts most

Where does staging count most? In six places. The entrance and hall, which create the first impression in person and in photographs. The living room, which buyers use to judge the whole home. The kitchen, where clear surfaces and clean lines sell space and quality. The main bedroom, which should feel calm and hotel-like. The bathrooms, which need to be fresh, white and spotless. And outdoor space, an extra room in warm climates and a lovely view in every climate. If an owner has a limited budget, start here.

Slide 15: The higher the price, the higher the expectation.

At the higher end of the market, buyers expect a property to look and feel finished. They are comparing it with other beautiful homes, and often they are buying from a distance. Quality staging, refined styling and excellent photography are not extras at this level. They are part of the standard you are expected to deliver.

Slide 16: Staging works wherever people buy

The principles of staging are the same wherever you sell. Consider two groups. Overseas buyers who purchase in your country often decide from photographs and video first. They compare your property with homes in their own country, and they need to see the space clearly and quickly. And nationals buying abroad want to picture a holiday or second home. Many never visit before they commit, so they respond to a home that looks welcoming and ready. The method is proven to work worldwide, and it is most valuable when the buyer cannot walk through the door before deciding.

Slide 17: Four things you may hear

You will hear misunderstandings about staging, from owners and sometimes from colleagues. My listing will sell itself. Some do, and staging helps the rest to stand out. Staging is only for empty homes. In fact occupied homes benefit from preparation too, often more. Staging is too expensive. A consultation is a low-cost way in, and the owner chooses the scale. And buyers want to see the real home. They do, and staging lets them see its potential clearly. Notice the tone of the replies: calm, respectful and never argumentative. We build on them in module two.

Slide 18: Module one takeaways

Let's recap module one. Staging prepares a property for the widest range of buyers, and it never hides problems. There are four types: consultation, occupied, vacant and virtual. It works at every step of the buyer's journey, from the first photograph to the final decision. Quote evidence carefully, because agents' reports are not guarantees. And the method works worldwide, especially for buyers who decide from a distance. Now let's put it to work in your marketing.

Slide 19: Using Staging as a Marketing Tool

Module two: Using Staging as a Marketing Tool. Now we turn understanding into action: where staging fits in your sales process, how to raise it with a seller, how to handle objections, how to prepare for photography and viewings, and how staging builds your own business.

Slide 20: Staging is part of your marketing

Think of staging as part of your marketing, not a separate service. It improves your photographs for every portal and brochure. It helps your listing stand out online and on social media. It makes viewings easier and more memorable. And it helps buyers feel confident enough to make an offer. When you present staging to a seller in these terms, you are not asking for extra work. You are showing them how their home will be marketed at its best.

Slide 21: You are the first professional the owner trusts.

In most sales, you are the first professional the owner trusts. They will ask you, what should I do before we list? That moment is an opportunity. If you have a clear, confident answer, and it includes staging, you help the owner and improve your own listing at the same time.

Slide 22: Where staging fits

Here is where staging fits in your sales process. At the valuation, notice the condition of the home and say what you see, kindly. At the listing presentation, show how staging supports your marketing plan. At preparation, agree a plan with the owner and book the stager before the photographer. During marketing and viewings, photograph, publish and help the owner keep the home ready day by day. And at offers, report the feedback and the results. If you plan the staging first, everything after it runs more smoothly.

Slide 23: Three things to do in your presentation

In your listing presentation, do three things. Show: include a short staging page, photographs of homes you have marketed, and your preferred stager and their work. Explain: how staging improves photographs and viewings, the options and what each involves, and what it asks of the owner. And agree: who will do what, which dates matter and how you will report the results. In your pack you have a ready-made listing presentation page that you can adapt with your own branding and details.

Slide 24: How to raise staging with a seller

How do you raise staging with a seller? Use four steps. Observe: look at the home the way a buyer will. Ask: a question such as, what would you like buyers to feel when they walk in? Explain: describe the benefit in terms of their sale, not your preferences. And offer: give two or three clear options rather than one big decision. Owners respond well to choice and to a calm, respectful tone. The conversation guide in your pack gives you wording for each step.

Slide 25: The words you choose

The words you choose matter, because a home is deeply personal. Try, let's help buyers picture themselves here. Or, what would make this home photograph at its best? Or, let's prepare it for the widest range of buyers. Avoid, your home is a mess. Avoid, buyers won't like your taste. And avoid, you need to get rid of all your things. Always talk about the buyer and the sale, never about the owner's choices. It lets the owner agree without feeling judged.

Slide 26: Three options for the owner

Offer the owner three options. Do it themselves: using your seller readiness checklist, they declutter, clean, repair and refresh. This costs least and takes the most effort. A professional consultation: a stager visits and writes a plan, and the owner or a team carries it out. A good balance of cost and impact. And full staging: a stager prepares and styles the property, with furniture and accessories where needed. It gives the most impact and asks the least effort from the owner. Options put the owner in control, which makes a yes much easier.

Slide 27: When to call a professional

Many owners can start today without a professional. Clear surfaces and personal photographs, deep clean, fix small repairs and replace tired bulbs, open the curtains, add light and tidy the garden. These cost little and make a visible difference. But recommend a professional when the home is vacant or very full, when the layout confuses buyers, or when the property is high in value or aimed at overseas buyers. Knowing the difference makes you a trusted adviser and keeps expectations realistic.

Slide 28: Handling objections

Owners will raise worries, and that is normal. It costs too much: offer a consultation first, so the owner chooses the scale. My home is already nice: agree, then show how buyers will see it, neutral, light and uncluttered. I will not get the money back: do not promise anything, but share what agents report and your own results. The objection cards in your pack give you short, respectful answers to each. Treat every worry as a question, answer it simply and keep listening.

Slide 29: More objections

More worries you will hear. Buyers will see through it. Staging is honest: it shows the home at its best and hides nothing. It will take too long. Plan the dates together, because most of the work happens before photography. And, I will do it myself. That is great. Hand over the checklist and offer to review it. In every case, stay calm and stay on the owner's side. You are not selling a service. You are helping them sell their home.

Slide 30: Who pays for staging

Who pays? Practice varies by market and agency. Commonly the owner pays the stager directly. Some agencies include staging in a marketing package. And developers often budget for staging as part of the sale. Whatever the arrangement, check your agency's policy, check local rules on fees, referrals and disclosure, and make sure everyone agrees in writing. Rules differ from country to country, so please take local advice before agreeing any commercial arrangement.

Slide 31: Prepare first. Then photograph.

The photographs will be seen far more often than the home itself. So the order matters: prepare the home, stage it, then book the photographer. Never the other way round. A good photographer can do wonders, but they can only photograph what is there.

Slide 32: Photography-day checklist

On photography day, check six things. Repairs done: fix and finish before the photographer arrives. Deep clean: windows, floors, kitchen and bathrooms. Declutter: clear counters, shelves and surfaces. Style: cushions, linen, lamps and a touch of greenery. Light: every bulb works, curtains open and lamps on. And outside: a tidy entrance, garden and any terrace. The full checklist is in your pack. Agree the date with the owner well in advance, because a rushed shoot wastes your best marketing opportunity.

Slide 33: Ready on every viewing day

A home that looks wonderful in photographs must also look wonderful on the day, and viewings can be at short notice. Help the owner with a simple routine, and think of the senses. Sight: beds made, surfaces clear and curtains open. Smell: fresh and neutral, so open the windows and avoid strong scents. Sound: quiet, or soft background music. Light: every lamp on, even in the daytime. Temperature: comfortable, warm in winter and cool in summer. And people and pets: where possible out of the way, so buyers can speak freely.

Slide 34: Match the approach to the property

Match the approach to the property. A vacant home needs furniture in the key rooms, so buyers understand scale and purpose. An occupied home needs decluttering, depersonalising and styling with what is there. A new build needs to show how the space can be lived in and to feel warm. A luxury home needs refined styling and photography with the highest finish. And a rental or holiday let needs durable furnishings that are welcoming and photographed to attract bookings. Once you can match approach to property, you can recommend with confidence.

Slide 35: Selling to buyers at a distance

Buyers at a distance rely on what you give them. That includes overseas buyers purchasing in your country and nationals buying abroad. Prepare strong photographs and, if possible, video, clear room-by-room descriptions and floor plans with room labels. Then support them: respond across time zones, offer live video tours and introduce trusted local professionals. A staged home is much easier to understand on a screen, which is a real advantage when the buyer cannot visit before deciding.

Slide 36: Virtual staging, used well

Virtual staging has its place. It is useful for marketing images of vacant homes, for showing possible layouts and for reaching buyers who search online first. But always label it clearly as virtual, because visitors must never be misled, and some markets have rules about this. And real staging gives a better viewing, because buyers experience the space, not an image of it. Think of virtual staging as one tool in the kit, not a replacement for preparing the home.

Slide 37: What staging gives your business

What does staging give your own business? Six things. Stand out: win the listing with a clear plan for presenting it. Better marketing: stronger photographs and content for every channel. Happier sellers: owners who feel guided and cared for. Smoother sales: fewer surprises at viewings and at offer stage. Referrals: pleased owners and stagers who recommend you. And professional profile: you become known as the agent who presents homes well. None of this depends on a single sale. It builds, listing after listing.

Slide 38: Be known as the agent who prepares properties well.

Staging becomes powerful when it becomes part of who you are as an agent. Be known as the agent who prepares properties well. Share your results, show your process and make presentation part of your marketing story. Post before-and-after pictures with the owner's permission, talk about your approach in listing presentations and mention your partner stagers. Over time, owners and other professionals will think of you first.

Slide 39: Module two takeaways

Let's recap module two. Staging is part of your marketing: photographs, viewings and offers. You are the first professional the owner trusts, so be ready with a clear answer. Raise it in four steps: observe, ask, explain, offer. Offer options, answer worries calmly and talk about the buyer and the sale. And prepare and stage first, then book the photographer. Slide 40: Partnering with Stagers

Module three: Partnering with Stagers. A good partnership makes every listing easier. We look at how to find and check a professional, how to brief them, who does what, how to agree arrangements in writing and how to measure what staging does for you.

Slide 41: You are the sales expert. The stager is the presentation expert.

Why partner with a stager rather than doing it yourself? You are the sales expert. The stager is the presentation expert. Together you serve the owner better. You stay in your role: you sell, and the stager prepares and styles. You make faster decisions, because a trusted partner means a clear plan and a quick start. And you deliver a consistent standard, so every listing is presented the way you want it to be. A good partnership saves you time and strengthens your reputation.

Slide 42: Where to find a professional

Where do you find a professional stager? Start with GAPHS. Our online directory lists members, and local chapters connect professionals in their region where chapters are available. Beyond that, review portfolios of recent projects and ask for referrals from photographers, developers and other agents. And begin with a trial project: work on one listing together before you refer widely. A trial shows you how they communicate, how they handle deadlines and how owners respond to them.

Slide 43: What GAPHS membership tells you

What does a GAPHS membership tell you about a stager? First, formal training: membership is open only to people who have completed a home staging training course. Second, ethical practice: GAPHS is built on education, ethics and excellence. Third, continuing development: members have access to ongoing training and webinars. And fourth, a public profile: members can showcase their work in the directory. Membership is a helpful starting point, not a replacement for your own checks, so always review the portfolio and speak with references.

Slide 44: What to look for

What should you look for? Six things. Portfolio: recent work at the standard you need. Process: a clear, written way of working. Insurance: appropriate cover for the work and the stock. References: agents and owners who will speak for them. Reliability: they meet dates and communicate early. And fit: a style and tone that suit your clients. The vetting questions in your pack turn this into a short interview you can use with any stager before you refer them to a seller.

Slide 45: Questions to ask before you refer

Here are questions to ask before you refer a stager to a seller. About the work: which services do you offer? Can I see three recent projects? How do you price, and what is included? About how they work: how long from first visit to ready? What do you need from the owner? Are you insured, and can you show it? Take notes, compare answers if you speak to more than one stager, and trust your instincts. You are lending your reputation to this person.

Slide 46: What to expect from a professional

What should you expect from a professional? Before: a visit and a written plan, a clear quote and timeline, and agreed responsibilities. During: regular updates to you and the owner, work completed to the agreed date and respect for the home and its owner. After: a photography-ready property, a plan for removal or maintenance and feedback on viewings. If a stager cannot give you these, keep looking.

Slide 47: Briefing a stager

A good brief makes everything easier. Share six things. The property: address, size, condition and whether it is vacant or occupied. The buyer: who you expect to buy, including overseas buyers. Price and budget: the price band and what the owner is willing to invest. The dates: the listing date and the photography date. Access: keys, contacts, parking and the owner's availability. And marketing: where the home will be shown, so the stager can plan the shots. The staging brief template in your pack puts all of this on one page.

Slide 48: Working back from photography day

Plan the sequence by working back from photography day. Agree the plan, with you, the owner and the stager. Prepare: repairs, painting and a deep clean come first. Style: the stager installs furniture and accessories. Photograph: book the photographer once the home is ready. Launch: list, publish and begin viewings. Allow enough time for each stage, and ask the stager for their timeline early. Rushing is the most common cause of disappointing listing photographs.

Slide 49: Who does what

Clear roles prevent misunderstandings. The agent is responsible for the sale, the marketing plan and the timeline. The stager is responsible for the assessment, the plan, the styling and the installation. The owner is responsible for decisions, repairs, access and keeping the home ready. And the photographer captures the finished home at its best. When everyone knows their role, the process feels calm and the owner feels looked after.

Slide 50: How agents and stagers work together

How do agents and stagers work together? Models vary. In a preferred stager arrangement, you recommend a professional and the owner hires them. In a marketing package, staging is part of your offer. And there are joint projects for developers and portfolios. Whichever model you use, be transparent. Tell owners about any financial interest, never recommend a stager only for a fee, and follow local rules on referral fees, which differ widely from country to country. When in doubt, seek local legal advice.

Slide 51: Put the arrangement in writing

Put your arrangement in writing. Cover six points. Scope: what each party will do. Fees: who pays, how much and when. Dates: the visit, installation, photography and removal. Access and insurance: who holds keys and who carries cover. Disclosure: what the owner is told, and when. And changes and ending: how to change or end the arrangement. The partnership outline in your pack lists these points as a guide. It is not legal advice, so please seek local legal advice before you agree anything, as requirements differ by country.

Slide 52: Keeping the owner informed

Communication keeps owners confident. Before: explain the plan, the dates and what they need to do. During: send a short update with a photograph of progress. After: share the viewing feedback and the next steps. Owners rarely complain when they feel informed. When something does go wrong, they accept it far more readily if they heard it from you first.

Slide 53: What to track on every staged listing

What should you track? Days to first viewing: how quickly interest appears. Online views and saves: how the listing performs against others. Number of viewings, compared with similar listings. Viewing feedback: what buyers say about the presentation. Offers and price: the offers received and the final price. And days on market: from listing to accepted offer. Use the results tracker in your pack. Be honest in your comparisons: every property is different, so look for patterns across many listings, not single results.

Slide 54: Build case studies

Case studies are your best marketing. Before: photograph the home as it was, with the owner's permission. The plan: summarise what was changed and why. The result: show the photographs and the outcome. And the words: add the owner's comment, again with permission. Use the case study template in your pack. Keep claims honest and specific, and share your case studies in presentations, on your website and on social media.

Slide 55: Build your network of stagers

As your confidence grows, build a small network. Cover your needs: one stager for occupied homes, one for vacant and luxury homes and a back-up for busy periods. Then keep it healthy: review results together every few months, share feedback openly and introduce stagers to your colleagues. A network of two or three trusted professionals is worth far more than a long list of names.

Slide 56: Partnering across borders

The partnership can reach across borders. Overseas buyers want the same standard they would see at home. Agents refer clients between countries. And GAPHS gives you a way to find professionals abroad, through its directory and chapters. Adopt good habits: agree language and time zones, share the brief in writing, and check the local rules in each country. A staged home helps buyers who cannot visit, and a trusted stager helps you deliver it wherever the property is.

Slide 57: When things go wrong

Even good partnerships hit problems: a late delivery, an owner who changes their mind or damage to an item. Prevent: agree dates and responsibilities in writing, check the home before installation and keep the owner informed. Respond: speak early, kindly and in person, record what happened and agree the next step together. Most problems are small if they are raised quickly. They only become large when no one speaks.

Let's recap module three. You sell, the stager prepares, and a trusted partner improves every listing. Vet stagers on their portfolio, process, insurance, references and reliability.
